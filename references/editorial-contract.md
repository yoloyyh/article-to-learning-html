# Editorial contract

## Evidence and coverage

Distinguish: (1) source-derived statements, (2) original teaching interpretation and labeled hypothetical examples, and (3) unresolved or unreviewed material. Build a compact internal map of claim → read section → qualification → source link or file and section label. Cite close to each substantial source-derived claim; a source list alone is not enough. Do not invent anchors.

Capture title, author, date and revision only if visible in the source. For aggregation, preserve both original and aggregator attribution when read; do not imply inaccessible sources were reviewed. Cite numerical values with their actual populations, dates, measurement methods and conditions. If an essential table, illustration or section is missing, make that limitation explicit and avoid inferring its contents.

For partial access, cover only the portion actually read; make the restriction conspicuous at the top and in the source description. A title or short snippet is not sufficient evidence for a full lesson. An abstract is sufficient for a limited abstract-only explanation if the user accepts or requests that scope. Never present a partial reading as a full-article analysis.

## Translation and summary

Follow applicable host rules and established permission. Clearly identify a faithful translation of the permitted supplied scope versus a concise non-substitutive summary of a source. Preserve numbers, caveats, uncertainties and technical distinctions. A summary must not masquerade as a complete translation; if the user asks for a full translation but it is unavailable or impermissible, explain the limitation and offer the permitted work. For Chinese sources, provide a source summary without pretending to translate.

The opening orientation and source summary may precede a long translation. Keep a full permitted translation in its own clearly labeled section, reachable from the opening; do not silently omit sections. Teaching examples and recommendations must be labeled and separated from the author's claims.

## Beginner explanation

- Explain the actual question, relevant objects and central relationship before jargon. Define essential terms in plain Chinese on first use and retain useful original terminology.
- Preserve causal direction, evidence quality, time boundary, negations and qualifiers such as “may,” “often” and “in this setting.” Attribute viewpoints and commercial claims.
- Choose the relevant conceptual model for the subject. For detection, distinguish the target, observable evidence and prediction; for product releases, distinguish what changed, product relationships and access conditions; for history, identify actors, events and context. Do not invent undisclosed mechanisms.
- Explain difficult ideas with concrete original examples when useful, including their limits. Do not present toy simulations as working systems or quantitative evidence. Select examples and exercises for learning value, not to fill a mandatory slot.
- Select representations appropriate to the content. Routes select branches; independent work can fan out and in; feedback returns to a revision step. If the source is not procedural, do not force a flowchart or invent an ending/failure branch.

## Before delivery

Can a beginner identify the article's question, main answer and conditions? Is each central assertion supported by a read section? Are source and added interpretation visibly separate? Are coverage, dates, quotations, translation mode and numbers accurate? Are important inaccessible parts disclosed? Do not sacrifice essential qualifications to achieve a visual ratio.
